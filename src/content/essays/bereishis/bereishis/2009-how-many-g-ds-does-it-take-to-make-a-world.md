Moses was not happy.  There he was, taking dictation from the Author of the Torah, writing each word per G-d's instructions.  They were up to Genesis, 1:21.  G-d told him to write, " . . . *and G-d said, let Us make man in Our image* . . . " *..in **Our** image* . . . ??? Moses was troubled by G-d referring to Himself in plural.  A major tenet of the Jewish faith is that there is only one G-d, who has no partners.  "Master of the World! You are giving an opening to heretics!" G-d explained to Moses that by "*Us*" He was referring to Himself and the angels. He was actually consulting them and asking their "permission" to create man. He wanted their input. Why did G-d feel the need to consult the angels?  Surely He knew what to do without asking them.  The *[Midrash](http://www.ou.org/about/judaism/m.htm#midrash)* explains that G-d wanted to teach us a very important lesson. We all have our priorities in life.  We have goals, hopes, and dreams.  Sometimes we tend to pursue those dreams with such single-mindedness that we forget that there are other people who may be affected by our decisions. Take, for example, the time my son and I came home from the lake with two large-mouth bass.  Until we could decide what to do with them, we determined that the best way to keep them fresh would be to let them swim in the bathtub. My wife very nicely asked me for some indication as to when the family could expect to get their bathtub back! Man does not live in a vacuum.  We share this planet with billions of other people.  Our choices affect others. It behooves us to take that into account before we make decisions. But what about Moses' objection?  What about the fact that people might misinterpret "*Let Us make man*" as an indication that G-d didn't do it all Himself?  What about the fact that this verse might encourage people to reject monotheism and accept non-Torah philosophies? "Write," said G-d to Moses. "Whoever wants to misinterpret will misinterpret." (*Midrash Rabbah*, 8:8) What is worse, heresy or ignoring the needs of your fellow man?  "Write," said G-d. True, there was a chance that an important theological concept would be distorted by those who were looking for an excuse to distort.  But the possibility that people would disregard the feelings of others was not a chance He was willing to take. One great Sage (he was quoted to me recently, but I don’t remember who it was), when analyzing this observation of the Midrash, summed it up this way: “The idea that G-d required assistance from other “gods” to help Him create the world is heretical.  The idea that the Torah finds it acceptable to act in a fashion that does not show concern for the feelings of others is also heretical!” Have a great Shabbos. Rabbi Yerachmiel Seplowitz
